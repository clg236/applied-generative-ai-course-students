# Should we send this email to professor? · A New icerynk Sim

## What it teaches

**After this run, a player can judge whether an email to a professor is ready to send by checking whether it is specific, respectful, and actionable.**

## Overview

This is a 15-minute simulation in which a student needs to contact a professor about an academic issue. The room reviews an AI-generated email and decides whether it is appropriate to send, needs revision, or should not be sent. The simulation focuses on evaluating AI-generated communication rather than simply generating it.

## Goals

- Help players evaluate whether an AI-generated email to a professor is appropriate to send.
- Encourage players to consider context, tone, and supporting details before sending AI-generated communication.
- End the simulation with one communication decision that players can clearly justify.

## Non-Goals

- Teaching students how to write professional emails from scratch.
- Teaching general English writing or grammar.
- Determining whether a student's request (e.g., an extension or grade appeal) should be approved.

## Audience

The primary audience is university students communicating with professors about academic matters such as extension requests, missed classes, office hours, or grade inquiries. Many already rely on AI to draft emails, but they need practice deciding whether AI-generated communication is appropriate before sending it.

## Existing solutions and issues

- **Generative AI Chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT):** Many students use generative AI tools to draft emails to professors. While these tools produce fluent and professional-sounding messages, they cannot determine whether an email is appropriate for the specific academic context or whether it should be sent at all.
- **Online Email Templates:** Students often search for sample emails or templates online. These examples provide useful language but are generic and do not encourage students to think about whether the request, timing, or tone is appropriate for their own situation.

## Assumptions

- Players have basic experience writing emails to professors and understand common academic situations, such as requesting an extension or asking for clarification.
- Players are familiar with generative AI tools and may already use them to draft academic emails.
- Players often assume that fluent AI-generated writing is appropriate without evaluating context, tone, or timing.
- Practicing realistic scenarios with feedback is an effective way to improve communication judgment.

## Constraints

- Runs entirely in a web browser with no installation required.
- Focuses on one realistic academic scenario and one communication decision.
- Can be completed in 10–15 minutes, including the debrief.
- Designed for teams of 2–5 players.
- All essential information is presented on a single screen to minimize unnecessary navigation.
- Requires no outside research or prior knowledge beyond the information provided in the simulation.

## Key use cases

**Evaluate AI-generated communication.** Players review an AI-generated email written to a professor about an academic issue, such as requesting an extension or clarifying a course policy. Instead of editing the email immediately, they first evaluate whether the message is appropriate based on its tone, context, and completeness. The simulation encourages players to focus on judgment rather than fluent writing.

**Select the appropriate communication strategy.** After reviewing the email and the surrounding context, players decide whether the AI-generated email should be sent as-is, revised before sending, or not sent at all. Their decision must be supported by evidence from the scenario rather than personal preference.

**Reflect on the decision.** During the debrief, players compare their decision with recommended communication practices and discuss why the AI-generated email was or was not appropriate. The simulation reinforces that effective communication depends on context and judgment, not just well-written text.

## The run

| Beat | Minutes | What the room sees | What the room does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 3 | An academic scenario (e.g., requesting an assignment extension), the course syllabus, the professor's communication policy, and an AI-generated email draft. | Read the scenario, identify the purpose of the email, and discuss whether the situation requires contacting the professor. |
| 2 | 7 | The AI-generated email, assignment details, previous communication (if any), and three possible actions: Send, Revise, or Don't Send. | Evaluate the email based on tone, context, completeness, and course policies. Make one team decision. |
| 3 | 5 | The recommended solution with explanations, examples of effective academic communication, and the consequences of each choice. | Compare the team's decision with the recommendation and discuss what the AI did well and what required human judgment. |

**The limit.** One decision. The team may choose only one action: Send, Revise, or Don't Send. Once the decision is submitted, it cannot be changed, and the team must defend its reasoning during the debrief.

**The tempting wrong move.** Spending most of the discussion polishing grammar and wording instead of asking whether the AI-generated email should be sent at all. A perfectly written email is still the wrong choice if the request is inappropriate or the context is missing.

**The endings.**
- **Send.** The team concludes that the email is appropriate for the situation and explains why it is ready to send.
- **Revise before sending.** The team identifies specific weaknesses in the AI draft and improves it before sending.
- **Don't send.** The team decides that emailing the professor is not the right action at this time and explains what information or circumstances should change before sending.

## Research

### Domain research

**WHAT MAKES AN ACADEMIC EMAIL APPROPRIATE?**

An appropriate email has a clear subject line, explains the purpose concisely, provides enough context for the professor to understand the situation, and ends with a specific request. Politeness is important, but appropriateness depends just as much on timing, completeness, and whether the email should be sent at all. This became the evaluation criteria used throughout the simulation.

### Model research

**WHERE DID THE MODEL NEED ADDITIONAL HUMAN JUDGMENT?**

The model struggled to evaluate course-specific context, such as whether the answer was already available in the syllabus, whether the request followed the professor's communication policy, or whether the student's request was reasonable. Human judgment was needed to decide whether the email should be sent, not simply how it should be written.

## Part 7 - Why this beats just asking AI

### The bare prompt

*"Design a 15-minute team simulation that teaches university students how to decide whether an AI-generated email to a professor should be sent."* (write in pdf)

### What it produced

### Where the Output Fell Short

| AI output or behavior | What is wrong with it | How I know |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *"Edit the email together."* | This changes the main activity from deciding whether the AI-generated email should be sent into rewriting the email. Players are rewarded for editing skills instead of communication judgment. | My learning objective is deciding whether an AI-generated email is ready to send, needs revision, or should not be sent. The assignment also emphasizes one capability and one decision. |
| *"Score it using the rubric."* | The design suggests that adding up points determines the correct answer. In reality, deciding whether to email a professor depends on context, course policies, timing, and the nature of the request, which cannot be reduced to a numerical score. | My simulation asks players to justify their decision using evidence from the scenario rather than a point total. |
| *"Discuss disagreements and identify revisions."* | The model assumes that revising the email is the natural next step. It never encourages players to consider whether the email should be sent at all or whether the issue could be resolved without contacting the professor. | My simulation treats Send, Revise, and Do Not Send as equally valid outcomes depending on the academic context. |

### What I supplied that it could not

I supplied academic context that the model could not infer from a generic prompt, including course communication policies, assignment deadlines, and realistic professor–student expectations. I also changed the focus from editing AI-generated text to evaluating whether the email should be sent in the first place. Finally, I designed three equally valid outcomes: Send, Revise before sending, and Do Not Send. So, players practice judgment instead of assuming every situation requires an email.

### The correction log

| The model proposed | I changed it to | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Edit the email together | Evaluate whether the AI-generated email should be sent | The learning objective is judgment, not writing. |
| Score the email using a rubric | Discuss contextual evidence before making one decision | Communication decisions depend on context, not numerical scores. |
| Team roles focused on reviewing and editing | Team discussion focused on defending one communication decision | Encourages evidence-based reasoning instead of collaborative rewriting. |

### The test a reader can run

Compare the two simulations for two minutes. In the model's version, players spend part of the activity editing and scoring the email using a rubric. In my version, players spend the activity deciding whether the AI-generated email should be sent at all and defend The Run section.
